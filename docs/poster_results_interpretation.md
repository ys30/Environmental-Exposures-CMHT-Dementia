# Poster discussion: empirical findings versus proposed mechanisms

This note integrates the research interpretation developed for the dementia/environmental-exposure poster **without uploading the poster or its graphics**. It is supplementary to [manuscript-reported findings](manuscript_results.md), which remain the source of the approximate numeric estimates. This note is **not** a new model run, statistical verification, or disclosure approval.

## Empirical findings currently documented

- **Population and outcome:** South London SLaM CRIS cohort of older adults with dementia (reported N = 5,007); CMHT contact counts at reported follow-up windows, modeled with observation-time offsets.
- **Greenness (NDVI):** manuscript-reported adjusted IRRs approximately 0.38, 0.32 and 0.33 for Years 1, 5 and 9, respectively, in the PM2.5-adjusted specification. These estimates are **per one full NDVI unit** and should not be interpreted as the size of a typical neighborhood greenness contrast.
- **Temperature:** approximate adjusted IRR 1.02 per 1°C in a reported model; this is **not** an estimate of the effect of an additional extreme-heat day. Exact exposure definitions and follow-up window need verification.
- **Deprivation (IMD):** approximate adjusted IRRs 1.010 (Year 1) and 1.007 (Years 5 and 9) per IMD point. These are **main effects**, not evidence that deprivation significantly modifies the PM2.5 association.
- **Air pollutants:** PM2.5, PM10, NO2 and NOx were analyzed, but the exact pollutant-quartile estimates have not been verified in this repository.
- **Interactions:** pollution × NDVI, pollution × temperature and pollution × IMD were considered; available summaries characterize many interactions as statistically inconclusive. No confirmed buffering, heat synergy, or deprivation amplification should be claimed without the model-specific interaction coefficient, uncertainty interval and test.

## Conceptual framework (hypotheses, not confirmed findings)

| Hypothesis | Relationship to evaluate | Plausible mechanism; not tested directly here |
|---|---|---|
| H1: environmental main associations | Higher PM2.5 and heat exposure, and lower greenness, may be associated with more CMHT contacts | Pollution: oxidative stress and neuroinflammation; heat: disrupted sleep and thermoregulatory stress; greenness: psychological restoration and reduced stress |
| H2: PM2.5 × NDVI | Greenness may attenuate the pollution–CMHT association | Restoration, social engagement, and possible reduction of environmental stress |
| H3: PM2.5 × heat | Heat may strengthen the pollution–CMHT association | Overlapping inflammatory, cardiovascular and physiological stress |
| H4: PM2.5 × IMD | Deprivation may modify the pollution–CMHT association | Unequal baseline vulnerability, housing conditions, care access and protective resources |

**Borough is not interchangeable with IMD.** Borough-level variation in the PM2.5 association, if tested, requires a separate PM2.5 × borough analysis; it cannot be inferred from an IMD main effect or an IMD interaction. Borough may also represent geographic differences in service organization and access, not only socioeconomic deprivation.

## Wording suitable for a public research summary

> Manuscript-reported analyses suggest associations between neighborhood greenness, temperature, socioeconomic deprivation and CMHT service-use counts among older adults with dementia. Pollution-related effect modification by greenness, heat and deprivation remains to be confirmed from the original interaction estimates. Proposed pathways include neuroinflammation, psychological restoration, heat-related physiological stress and social vulnerability; these mechanisms were not directly measured.

## Interpretation and limitations

CMHT utilization is an indicator of **recorded service contact**, not a direct measure of symptom severity or unmet need. Higher or lower counts may reflect service access, clinical need, referral patterns and observation time. The retrospective observational design does not establish causality; postcode-based exposure assignment may misclassify personal exposure; unmeasured care support, mobility and housing conditions may confound estimates; and generalizability beyond the South London service setting is uncertain.

## Verification before stronger claims

1. Retrieve the approved final model tables and document exposure units, analytic N, covariate adjustment, IRRs, 95% CIs and p-values.
2. Check whether extreme heat was modeled as days above a threshold or whether results reflect continuous temperature; do not conflate these measures.
3. Audit PM2.5 × NDVI, PM2.5 × heat, PM2.5 × IMD and, if applicable, PM2.5 × borough terms, including joint tests for categorical interactions.
4. Distinguish model-supported associations from plausible biological or psychosocial explanations.
5. Confirm that any additional aggregate results are approved for public release before adding them.
